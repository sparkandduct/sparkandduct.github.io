How it is worked out

kVA = √3 × V × I ÷ 1000 for three-phase, with V measured between two phase legs.

kW = kVA × power factor and I = kVA × 1000 ÷ (√3 × V)

kVAR = √(kVA² − kW²)

Single-phase drops the √3 (1.732): kVA = V × I ÷ 1000. These assume a balanced load, with the same current on all three legs.

Worked example: a 480 V three-phase load draws 100 A at 0.85 power factor. kVA = 1.732 × 480 × 100 ÷ 1000 = 83.14 kVA. kW = 83.14 × 0.85 = 70.67 kW. Going the other way, 70.67 kW at 0.85 needs 70,670 ÷ (1.732 × 480 × 0.85) = 100 A.

Using it in the field

  • Heating elements are resistive, so use a power factor of 1. A 15 kW electric heat strip on 208 V three-phase draws 15,000 ÷ (1.732 × 208) = 41.6 A per leg.
  • Motors and compressors run at a power factor below 1, often 0.8 to 0.9 when loaded, lower when lightly loaded. This is a typical range, not a fixed value. The calculator gives the power the load takes from the line, not the shaft output, which is lower by the motor efficiency.
  • Sizing wire and breakers uses the nameplate full-load or rated-load amps and the code rules, not a computed current. Use this page to check a reading or estimate a load. Check the wire with the voltage drop calculator.
  • Check the leg balance. Measure all three legs with a clamp meter. If they differ by more than a few percent, the formula above is only an estimate. A single-phase load on a three-phase panel is not a three-phase load.
  • For a single load, Ohm’s law still works per leg when the load is resistive.
