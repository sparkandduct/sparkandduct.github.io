Electrical and HVAC licensing in North Carolina

Who issues what, and how much experience you need before you can sit the exam.

Rules and fees change. This page is a summary checked against the boards' published rules in October 2026; confirm the details with the board before you apply.

Contractors are licensed, not individual workers

North Carolina licenses the contractor: the person or business that offers the work to the public. Someone working as an employee under a licensed contractor's supervision does not need their own state license, although the hours they work count toward one later.

Electrical

Electrical contracting is licensed by the State Board of Examiners of Electrical Contractors. There are three general classifications and several special restricted ones.

ClassificationExperience to qualifyScope
Limited2 years, at least 1 primarySmaller projects, capped by project value and voltage
Intermediate4 years, at least 2½ primaryLarger projects, with a higher value cap
Unlimited5 years, at least 4 primaryNo project value limit

"Primary" experience is hands-on electrical installation work or directly supervising it. The remainder can be secondary experience such as related education or estimating. The dollar caps for the limited and intermediate classes are set by the state and have been raised over time, so read the current figures on the board's site.

The qualifying exam requires a score of at least 70 out of 100, and the board's rules list a $90 application and examination fee.

Fire alarm and low voltage

Low voltage work has its own special restricted classification, SP-FA/LV, which covers fire alarm, burglar alarm and low voltage wiring. It requires 2 years of experience, at least 1 of them primary. The other special restricted classifications cover single-family dwellings, elevators, plumbing and heating equipment wiring, groundwater pumps, electric signs and swimming pools.

Heating and air conditioning

HVAC contracting is licensed by the State Board of Examiners of Plumbing, Heating and Fire Sprinkler Contractors. Heating licenses are divided into groups:

  • Heating Group 1 (H-1): water-based comfort heating systems, such as boilers and hydronic piping.
  • Heating Group 2 (H-2): forced-air heating and cooling systems over 15 tons.
  • Heating Group 3 (H-3): forced-air heating and cooling systems of 15 tons or less. This is the license most residential HVAC contractors hold.

To sit a contractor exam, the board asks for 2 years (4,000 hours) of on-site, full-time experience in the category. Up to half of that, 2,000 hours, can be academic or technical training directly related to the field.

The board also issues technician licenses, which let the holder work under a licensed contractor with less experience required: 3,000 hours for one class and 2,500 for the other, according to the board's applicant information.

EPA Section 608

Separately from any state license, federal law requires anyone who opens a refrigerant circuit or buys regulated refrigerant to hold EPA Section 608 certification. It never expires. The EPA 608 Core practice quiz covers the section every candidate has to pass.

A sensible order

  1. Work for a licensed contractor and keep a record of your hours and the kind of work.
  2. Get EPA 608 early if you are on the HVAC side; employers expect it.
  3. Check the board's current application form for exactly how experience must be documented.
  4. Apply, then prepare for the exam using the reference list the board publishes.
