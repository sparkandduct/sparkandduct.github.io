How to find a ground fault on a fire alarm panel

A patient, divide-and-conquer job. Here is the order that wastes the least time.

Before you start, tell the monitoring company and the building contact, and put the system on test. Disconnecting circuits can cause signals and will leave areas unprotected until you restore them.

What a ground fault is

Fire alarm circuits are meant to float, with no connection to earth ground. A ground fault trouble means some conductor has an unintended path to ground, usually through a conduit, back box or wet device. One fault often causes no symptoms, but a second fault on another conductor can disable a circuit, so panels are required to report the first one.

Step 1: Confirm it is real and steady

Check the panel's event history. A fault that comes and goes with rain, humidity or a particular time of day points to water or to equipment that only runs sometimes. Note whether the panel says positive or negative ground fault, if it tells you.

Step 2: Find which circuit

  1. Remove one field circuit at a time from the panel terminals: both conductors, and the shield if there is one.
  2. Wait. Many panels take up to a minute to clear or re-detect a ground fault.
  3. When the trouble clears, the circuit you just lifted has the fault. Label everything as you go so it goes back where it came from.

If the fault stays with every field circuit lifted, look at the panel itself: battery leads, a pinched harness, auxiliary power outputs or an add-on card.

Step 3: Confirm with a meter

With the faulted circuit disconnected at both ends, measure resistance from each conductor to a known ground such as the cabinet. A healthy conductor reads open. A reading of a few megohms or less shows which conductor is leaking. Panels can detect faults well above what a basic meter shows clearly, so an insulation tester helps on stubborn ones. Disconnect devices before using an insulation tester, since its test voltage can damage electronics.

Step 4: Halve the circuit

  1. Go to a device near the middle of the run and separate the wiring there.
  2. Measure each half to ground. The fault is in the half that still reads low.
  3. Go to the middle of that half and repeat.

Each split halves what is left, so a 40-device circuit takes about five or six stops.

Where faults usually are

  • A conductor pinched between a device and its back box, or under a mounting screw.
  • Insulation nicked where the cable enters a box or connector.
  • Water in an outdoor device, a rooftop duct detector or underground conduit.
  • A cable shield or drain wire touching a box, or grounded at both ends.
  • Staples or ties driven too tight, and damage from other trades above ceilings.
  • Anything that was worked on just before the trouble first appeared.

Finish properly

Repair the damage instead of just moving the wire, reconnect every circuit, confirm the panel is clear of troubles, test the devices on the circuit you opened, and take the system off test.
