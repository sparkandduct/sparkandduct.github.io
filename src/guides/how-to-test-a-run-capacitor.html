How to test a run capacitor and when to replace it

Power off, discharge, pull the wires, and read microfarads from C to each terminal. Replace it if the reading is outside the tolerance printed on the can, the top is bulged, or the meter reads open.

Short answer: a run capacitor is good when the reading is within its printed tolerance, usually ±6%. A 45 µF capacitor reads 42.3 to 47.7 µF. Anything lower, a bulged top, oil leaks or an open reading means replace it.

A capacitor can hold a lethal charge after the power is off. Open the disconnect, confirm zero volts with a meter, then discharge the capacitor before you touch the terminals.

Bench test with a capacitance meter

  1. Turn off the disconnect and the thermostat. Verify 0 V at the contactor and at the capacitor.
  2. Discharge the capacitor. A resistor of about 10,000 to 20,000 ohms across the terminals does it without a spark or a capacitor discharge tool. Many techs use an insulated screwdriver, which is common but controversial.
  3. Photograph the wiring, then pull the wires off the capacitor.
  4. Read the printed rating and tolerance on the can, for example 45/5 µF 370 V ±6%.
  5. Set the meter to capacitance. On a dual capacitor, put one lead on C and the other on HERM, and write down the reading. Move the second lead to FAN and read again. Never measure between HERM and FAN.
  6. Compare each reading with its tolerance window (table below).

Use the tolerance printed on the can. At least one maker lists ±6% as its standard; other sources quote ±5% or allow up to 10% low as a field rule, and the 10% column is there for that case. A reading right at the low edge is a judgment call; capacitance only drifts down with age.

What counts as a failed capacitor

  • Reading below the low limit on either section of a dual capacitor.
  • Meter reads open or zero.
  • Bulged top, oil on the case or terminals. Surface rust alone is not a reason.
  • A reading well above the rating is usually a bad connection or meter error. Reconnect the leads and read again.

Live test with the amp method

Done with the unit running, this gives microfarads from a clamp reading and a volt reading. It is live work. Do it only on the outdoor unit, never on an indoor blower with a spinning wheel next to the leads.

µF = start winding amps × 2,652 ÷ volts across the capacitor

Clamp the wire on the HERM terminal (the start winding wire), with the wire centered in the jaw. Read volts from HERM to C at the same time. The constant 2,652 is 1,000,000 ÷ (2 × π × 60), so it assumes 60 Hz.

Worked example: 7.8 A and 292.9 V gives 7.8 × 2,652 = 20,685.6, and 20,685.6 ÷ 292.9 = 70.6 µF. On a 70 µF capacitor that is within ±6% (65.8 to 74.2). If the live number comes out low, confirm with the bench test before you replace the part.

Choosing the replacement

  • Match the µF rating of each section. Do not round a 40 up to a 45; the motor and the capacitor are matched.
  • Equal or higher voltage rating. A 440 V part can replace a 370 V part. A 370 V part cannot replace a 440 V part. The marked voltage is a peak rating, not the voltage the circuit runs at.
  • Same shape and terminals; mount it upright with the terminals up.
  • Reconnect each wire to the terminal it came off, then check compressor and fan amps against the nameplate.
  • If capacitors keep failing, check for high line voltage, a weak contactor with pitted contacts, and a motor drawing high amps.
