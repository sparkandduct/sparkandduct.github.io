How it is worked out

superheat = suction line temperature − saturation temperature at suction pressure

subcooling = saturation temperature at liquid line pressure − liquid line temperature

Saturation temperature is the boiling or condensing temperature that goes with a pressure for that refrigerant. The calculator looks it up in a built-in pressure and temperature table (gauge pressure at sea level, every 2°F) and reads between rows. For example, R-410A at 118 psig saturates at about 40°F, so a suction line at 52°F is 12°F superheat; at 418 psig it saturates at about 120°F, so a liquid line at 110°F is 10°F subcooling.

R-410A boils and condenses over a temperature range of only a fraction of a degree, so one table serves both sides. The calculator does not cover blends with a large temperature glide, such as R-454B or R-407C; use the maker's bubble and dew point chart for those.

Using the numbers when charging

  • Fixed metering device (piston or capillary tube): charge to a target superheat. The target comes from the manufacturer's chart on the unit or in its manual and depends on indoor wet-bulb and outdoor dry-bulb temperature. Take the suction pressure and temperature near the service valve, and the wet-bulb reading in the return air before the coil.
  • Thermostatic expansion valve (TXV): charge to a target subcooling, taken at the liquid line near the outdoor unit. The value is on the data plate or in the installation manual and varies by model. Superheat on a TXV system is set by the valve, so use it to spot a problem rather than to add or remove charge.
  • Reading the result: high superheat with low subcooling points to a low charge or a restriction ahead of the metering device. Low superheat with high subcooling points to overcharge or poor airflow over the coil. Check airflow, filter and coil condition before touching the charge.
  • Measuring: put the thermometer clamp on bare, clean pipe and insulate over it, and let readings settle for several minutes after the system is running steadily. Recover and weigh refrigerant according to EPA Section 608 rules; never vent it.

Pressure and temperature relationships shift with altitude because gauges read relative to local air pressure, and the table here assumes sea level.
