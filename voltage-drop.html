How it is worked out

For a worked example, see how to calculate voltage drop on a NAC circuit.

drop = 2 × K × amps × feet ÷ circular mils, with 1.732 in place of 2 for three-phase. K is 12.9 for copper and 21.2 for aluminum.

The whole load is treated as sitting at the far end of the run, which is the cautious assumption for a notification circuit with devices spread along it. Reactance is ignored, so for large AC conductors use the NEC Chapter 9 Table 9 method instead.

Worked example: 16 A on 100 ft one way of 12 AWG copper (6,530 circular mils), single-phase. Drop = 2 × 12.9 × 16 × 100 ÷ 6,530 = 6.3 V, which is 5.3% of 120 V. That is over the 3% commonly used as a design target, so step up to 10 AWG (10,380 circular mils): 4.0 V, 3.3%.

Field tip: K comes from the Table 8 resistance of a conductor (ohms per 1,000 ft times its circular mils), so 12.9 is a rounded value for copper at 75 C. Warm or long runs measure slightly higher than the formula says.

Related: NAC voltage drop guide, box fill calculator, Ohm's law calculator.

The NAC preset starts at 20.4 V (85% of 24 V, the usual worst-case battery voltage) and requires 16 V at the last device. Use the figures from your panel and device data sheets if they differ.

Sources

  • NFPA 70 (NEC), Chapter 9 Table 8, conductor properties: circular mils and DC resistance per 1,000 ft, for 14 AWG and larger. Table 8 starts at 14 AWG; the 24 to 16 AWG areas (404, 642, 1,020, 1,620 and 2,580) are the standard AWG nominal values, which match the AWG diameter formula to within 0.3%.
  • IAEI Magazine, voltage drop formulas: K of 12.9 (copper) and 21.2 (aluminum) and the single-phase and three-phase formulas.
