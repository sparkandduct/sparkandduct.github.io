Electrical wire color codes (US)

What the colors normally mean in the United States, and which are required by code.

Never trust color alone. Test every conductor before you touch it.

ConductorColor
Phase A / Line 1Black
Phase B / Line 2Red
Phase CBlue
NeutralWhite
GroundGreen, green with a yellow stripe, or bare

ConductorColor

What the NEC actually requires

The phase colors above are trade convention, not code. The NEC itself fixes only a few colors:

  • White or gray is reserved for the grounded conductor, normally the neutral.
  • Green, green with yellow stripes, or bare is reserved for the equipment grounding conductor.
  • Orange marks the high leg on a four-wire delta system, where one phase measures about 208 V to neutral.
  • Where a building has more than one voltage system, each system's conductors must be identified and the scheme posted at the panels. Black-red-blue and brown-orange-yellow is how most buildings meet that.

Local rules, engineers' specifications and older buildings can all differ.

Switch legs and travelers

In cable, a white conductor used as a hot, for example in a switch loop, must be re-marked with tape or paint in a color other than white, gray or green. In older work it often was not, which is another reason to test.

Low voltage cable jackets

Jacket colors on low voltage cable are convention too, and vary by region and contractor:

  • Red: fire alarm.
  • Blue: data network, most commonly.
  • White or gray: security, access control or general purpose.
  • Yellow: often used for a separate network or for access control.

Read the print on the jacket for the cable's actual listing, such as FPLP or CMP, since the listing and not the color decides where it may be installed.
