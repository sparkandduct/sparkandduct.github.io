Ethernet T568A and T568B pinout

Hold the plug with the contacts up, the clip down and the plug end pointing away from you: pin 1 is on the left. T568A and T568B differ only in that the orange and green pairs trade places.

Short answer: use the same scheme at both ends of every cable run. Same scheme at both ends gives a straight-through cable. T568A on one end and T568B on the other gives a crossover. At a jack, follow the color code printed on the jack, not the plug table below.

Pair numbers follow the cabling standard: pair 1 is blue, pair 2 is orange, pair 3 is green, pair 4 is brown. Each pair is a solid wire and a white wire with a stripe of the same color. Blue and brown sit on pins 4 and 5, and 7 and 8, in both schemes.

Which scheme to use

Both work equally well electrically. The rule that matters is not to mix them in one run. Match what is already in the building. For new work, the TIA-568 standard recommends T568A for horizontal cabling, as summarized on Wikipedia, and T568B is allowed where it is needed to match existing T568B cabling. T568B is common in US commercial installs. Check the job spec.

Straight-through and crossover

CableOne endOther endUse
Straight-throughT568BT568B (or A and A)Computer to switch, jack to switch, camera to PoE switch.
Crossover (10/100)T568AT568BTwo like devices (switch to switch) when neither side has Auto-MDIX.

The A to B crossover swaps pairs 2 and 3: pin 1 goes to pin 3 and pin 2 goes to pin 6. It carries 10BASE-T and 100BASE-TX, which use only pins 1, 2, 3 and 6. Gigabit (1000BASE-T) uses all four pairs in both directions, so a fully crossed cable also swaps pairs 1 and 4 (pin 4 to 7, pin 5 to 8). Auto-MDIX, which swaps transmit and receive inside the port, was optional in the gigabit standard but is built into nearly all current switches and network cards, so a patch cable does the job. A crossover is rarely needed today.

Half the pairs are enough for 10/100, so a plug with only pins 1, 2, 3 and 6 good will link at 100 Mbps and fail at gigabit. Gigabit needs all four pairs. That is a classic cause of a link that drops to 100 Mbps.

Terminating tips

  • The Fiber Optic Association gives a limit of 0.5 inch (13 mm) of untwist for Cat 5, 5e, 6 and 6A. Strip only what you need and keep each pair twisted right up to the contacts or jack.
  • Jacks are wired to the code printed on the back of the jack. Cat 5, 5e and 6 jack internals can differ from the plug order, so follow that code and not memory.
  • Channel limits: 90 meters of installed horizontal cable and 100 meters total including patch cords, with no patch cord over 5 meters, per the TIA-568 summary. Longer runs are a different problem; see the PoE voltage drop and cable length calculator for the power side.

When the link will not come up

SymptomLikely wiring fault
No link light, tester shows an open pinConductor not seated or cut by the contact. Reterminate that end.
Links at 100 Mbps, not at 1 GbpsPair 1 or pair 4 open (pins 4, 5, 7 or 8).
No link, tester shows pins 1 and 3, 2 and 6 swappedA crossed cable (T568A on one end, B on the other) between two ports that cannot auto-correct.
Links but slow, with errors, and a continuity tester passesSplit pair (pins 3 and 6 wired as separate pairs, for example from wiring by color without pair order). A basic continuity tester does not catch it; a wire map tester that checks pairs does.

The last row is general practice, not taken from the sources below. If every wire tests good and the link still fails, check the cable length and the port.
