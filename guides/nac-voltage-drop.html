How to calculate voltage drop on a NAC circuit

Short answer: start from 20.4 V, subtract 16 V, and the wire may drop 4.4 V. Drop = 2 × 12.9 × amps × one-way feet ÷ circular mils. Use your panel maker's figures if they differ.

The voltage drop calculator has a fire alarm NAC preset that sets these numbers up for you.

Why it matters

Notification appliances are listed to work across a voltage range, commonly 16 to 33 V for 24 V devices. If wire resistance pulls the voltage at the far end below the bottom of that range, strobes can fall out of sync or fail to flash at the rated intensity. Inspectors ask for the calculation for that reason.

The two numbers that set the budget

  • Starting voltage: 20.4 V. The calculation assumes the worst case, which is the system running on batteries near the end of their discharge. That is taken as 85% of 24 V.
  • Minimum at the device: 16 V. This is the bottom of the listed range for most 24 V appliances. Check the data sheet.

That leaves 20.4 − 16 = 4.4 V that the wire is allowed to drop.

The lump-sum method

The simplest method treats every appliance as if it sat at the very end of the circuit. It overstates the drop, so a circuit that passes this way will pass in reality.

drop = 2 × K × amps × one-way feet ÷ circular mils

K is 12.9 for copper. The 2 accounts for the current going out and coming back.

Worked example

A circuit has horn strobes drawing 2.0 A in total, on 14 AWG copper (4,110 circular mils), with the last device 100 ft from the panel.

  • Drop: 2 × 12.9 × 2.0 × 100 ÷ 4,110 = 1.26 V
  • Voltage at the last device: 20.4 − 1.26 = 19.14 V, which passes.
  • Longest run this load allows on 14 AWG: 4.4 × 4,110 ÷ (2 × 12.9 × 2.0) = 350 ft.

If it fails

  • Go up a wire size. 12 AWG has about 60% more copper than 14 AWG.
  • Split the circuit. Halving the current halves the drop.
  • Move the power closer. A remote NAC power supply near the load shortens the run.
  • Use the point-to-point method. Calculating the drop segment by segment, with the current falling after each device, gives a lower and more accurate figure. Many panel manufacturers publish a spreadsheet for it.

Field tip: check the wire you actually pulled

Use the real run length from the plans plus the drops and risers, not the straight-line distance. Dead-end loops on a Class B circuit still count the full length to the last device. After install, measure at the last device with the horns sounding on battery power if the panel allows a test; a reading near 16 V means no margin for aging batteries.

Use the real currents

Take each appliance's current at the candela setting you will use, and at the voltage the data sheet specifies for calculations. Some manufacturers require their own starting voltage or method; where they do, theirs is the one the inspector will expect.

Sources

  • IAEI Magazine, voltage drop formulas: the single-phase formula and K of 12.9 for copper.
  • NFPA 70 (NEC), Chapter 9 Table 8: circular mils of 14 AWG (4,110) and DC resistance per 1,000 ft.
  • Fire alarm panel and notification appliance data sheets: the listed voltage range (commonly 16 to 33 V for 24 V devices) and the starting voltage to use. The 85% of 24 V starting point is a common design assumption; confirm the figure your panel maker requires.
