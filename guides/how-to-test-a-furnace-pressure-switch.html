How to test a furnace pressure switch

Put a manometer on the switch's tube and start the inducer. If the draft reaches the number printed on the switch and the contacts stay open, the switch is bad. If the draft falls short, the problem is the vent, the tubing or the inducer, and a new switch will not fix it.

Short answer: tubing first, inducer second, draft reading third, contacts last. Compare the reading with the closing pressure on the switch label, never with a number from the internet. Never jumper a pressure switch to make the furnace run: it is the proof that the flue is drawing, and bypassing it risks carbon monoxide in the house.

What the switch does

The switch is normally open. It is wired in the control board's safety circuit and tubed to the inducer housing or the heat exchanger. The board checks that it is open before it starts the inducer. When the inducer pulls enough draft, the diaphragm closes the contacts, and the board goes on to ignition. If the switch never closes, the board times out and sets a fault. HVACR School gives Carrier code 31 as an example, and the Goodman 3-flash code is the same fault.

Step 1: Look at the tubing with the power off

  • Pull the tube off the switch and the inducer or collector box and look through it. Check for cracks, dry rot, kinks, crud and water.
  • Make sure the tube seats fully on both barbs. A leak at either end means the switch cannot sense the draft.
  • Water in the tube means a condensate problem on a condensing furnace. Check the drain trap and hose before anything else.
  • Check the port on the inducer or collector box for rust or scale blocking it.

Step 2: Confirm the inducer runs right

  1. Call for heat and watch the inducer start. If it does not start, or hums without coming up to speed, the cause is the motor, its capacitor or the board, not the switch.
  2. With a meter, check supply voltage against the furnace data tag and the inducer amps against its nameplate. HVACR School warns of a motor problem if the amps climb steadily, or the motor gets noisy or too hot to touch.
  3. A motor that spins but does not reach rated speed cannot build enough draft to close the switch.

Step 3: Read the draft

  1. Tee the manometer into the tube, or pull the tube off the switch and put it on the manometer's negative port. Zero the meter first.
  2. Start the inducer and note the reading in inches of water column. It is a vacuum, so it will be negative.
  3. Read the closing pressure printed on the switch. Ferguson says setpoints are often in the range of 0.10 to 0.50 in. w.c. on standard units, but use the label. Two-stage furnaces have a low-stage and a high-stage switch, each with its own number.

Example: the label says −0.35 in. w.c. and the manometer reads −0.50 with the inducer at full speed. The draft is 0.15 above the closing point, so the switch should have closed. If its contacts are still open, replace it. If the reading were −0.20, the draft is short by 0.15 and the switch is right to stay open.

Step 4: Check the contacts

With the inducer running, set the meter to AC volts and measure from a good ground to each switch terminal. Ferguson's check: 24 V on the incoming terminal and 0 V on the outgoing one at full inducer speed means the switch is open. This assumes a 24 V safety circuit, which is common but not universal. Read the furnace wiring diagram first. A switch closed before the inducer starts is a fault too: HVACR School says that means a jumpered circuit, a short or a switch failed closed.

Finding a draft problem

  • Look at the vent and intake terminations for nests, debris and, on plastic pipe, ice. The Goodman GMH8 manual's troubleshooting table lists a blocked flue or weak inducer among its causes.
  • On a furnace that draws indoor air, run the house exhaust fans during the test. Ferguson says a depressurized house can cause sporadic lockouts.
  • High altitude can keep the switch from closing, so check that the furnace has the right switch or kit for the elevation, per Ferguson.
  • Replacement switches: use the original part or an adjustable universal switch. Ferguson says an adjustable one must be set with a manometer to the required value.

If the furnace locks out for other reasons after the switch closes, follow the no-ignition troubleshooting walkthrough. For limit trips, see how to measure temperature rise. For the board code, see Carrier or Goodman furnace codes.
